Plus 1.2 degrees. It is the most important number of our century and it sounds like nothing, roughly the difference you would not notice walking from one room into another. The problem with the global temperature record is not that people doubt it. The problem is that it does not feel like anything. I wanted to fix that.

## Why a spiral

A year is a loop. It starts in January, runs through summer and back to winter, and closes where it began. Most temperature charts cut that loop and lay it flat along a line, which is fine for reading exact values but throws away the thing everyone actually lives: the turning of a year, again and again.

So I kept the loop. The twelve months run clockwise around a circle, and each year is drawn as its own ring. A cool year sits close to the center. A warm year pushes outward. Stack a century and a half of them and the record stops being a line that ticks upward and becomes something that physically unwinds, coiling out from the middle and reddening as it goes.

## Saying it twice, on purpose

Each ring carries the temperature two ways at once: how far out it sits, and its color, from deep blue through a neutral gray at the historical average to deep red. Encoding the same thing twice is usually a mistake. Here it is the point. The eye catches warmth as color before it measures distance, and the two readings reinforce each other. By the time you have looked for a second you already know the recent rings are both far out and dark red, and you did not have to read a single number to feel it.

The gray midpoint is the 1951 to 1980 average, drawn as a faint reference ring. Two more rings mark plus 1.0 and plus 1.5 degrees, the thresholds the Paris agreement is written around. For most of the twentieth century the rings coil tightly inside that gray baseline. From the 1980s on they pull away, and the last decade sits almost entirely outside plus 1.0, brushing the line we said we would not cross.

## Letting it wind out

The still image is striking, but the story is in the motion. So the spiral can be played, one year laid down after another from 1880 forward. Watching it, the early decades wobble in place near the center, cold years and warm years cancelling out. Then, somewhere after 1980, the coil stops wobbling and starts to march. It is the difference between noise and a trend, and you see the exact moment one becomes the other.

## The same truth, three more ways

A spiral is good at feeling and bad at precision. You cannot read 1997 off it. So the page does not stop there. The same record is unrolled into one stripe per year, where the drift from blue to red is impossible to miss, and then drawn as a plain line, where the flat noisy century and the sharp bend after 1980 are laid out to measure. Different shapes answer different questions about the same numbers.

The last chart is the one that matters most. A warmer average is not a fact about the air, it is a fact about people. Heat waves kill, and the recorded death toll from them, the 2003 European summer, Russia in 2010, and the brutal cluster of 2022, 2023 and 2024, tracks the warming with a grim faithfulness. I was careful to say plainly what that data does and does not capture: heat deaths are badly under-counted, and the real toll runs far higher than any disaster ledger records.

## What I took away

The number was always true. What it lacked was a body. Wound into a spiral, plus 1.2 degrees stops being a statistic you nod at and becomes a shape that has clearly, visibly changed, and is still changing, in front of you. That is the whole job: not to make you believe the number, but to let you feel what it means.
